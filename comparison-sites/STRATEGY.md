# Bookkeeping software comparison site - strategy

**Status**: Proposed. Four owner decisions open.
**Owner**: Vincent, West Hill Ventures LLC
**Written**: 2026-08-05

---

## The decisions, up front

1. Build on **comparebookkeepingsoftware.com**. You own it. Skip the $995 for the other domain.
2. The asset is **a maintained dataset**, not a comparison site. The site, the reports, and the affiliate revenue are all views of one dataset.
3. **ClaryBook is never ranked first anywhere on the site**, and the ownership is disclosed site-wide. That rule is what makes the whole thing worth anything.
4. **Affiliate links only appear on pages ClaryBook does not compete for.** A ClaryBook customer is worth roughly 4x a QuickBooks affiliate payout, so the two must never sell against each other.
5. The reports are the main deliverable, not a side output. They are the only part that earns links, and links are the only thing that makes the rest possible.

My answer on the $995: no, not now. Google's 2012 exact-match-domain update removed most of the ranking benefit of having the keyword in the domain, so you would be paying for a memorable string, not for rankings. A URL path gets you the same keyword for free. And "best" in a domain you own becomes a liability the day you list your own product on it: a site literally called *best* bookkeeping software, run by a bookkeeping software company, is a headline waiting to happen. Revisit it defensively at 5k monthly sessions if a competitor starts sniffing around it.

Also worth knowing: comparebookkeepingsoftware.com still points at Squarespace parking, and westhillventures.com does too, despite the site being deployed to Railway back on 2026-07-31. The DNS gate from that project is still open. Same gate, two domains now.

## What this actually is

Not a comparison site. A maintained dataset of what bookkeeping tools cost and do, with a record of how that changed over time.

The distinction decides everything downstream. Comparison content is the most contested content on the internet: Forbes Advisor, NerdWallet, Capterra, G2, PCMag and Intuit's own pages all sit on the head terms with domain authority in the 80s and 90s. A new site does not out-rank them, and Google's 2026 core and spam updates have been landing hardest on exactly this genre.

But nobody maintains the underlying data. Forbes refreshes its listicle twice a year. Capterra prints what vendors give it. Nobody anywhere records that a given plan went from one price to another on a given date. That gap is the opening, and it is the one thing an agent fleet is genuinely better at than a media company: boring, high-frequency maintenance that no human wants to do.

The second reason this framing matters is where search is going. Answer engines cite structured, sourced, current data far more readily than they surface a new site in blue links. A dataset with dates and sources attached is the format they reach for. Chasing position 3 for "best bookkeeping software" is a fight you lose. Being the thing that gets quoted when someone asks what these tools cost is a fight nobody is having.

## Your three goals, ranked honestly

You named three: traffic to ClaryBook, affiliate revenue, and reports others republish. They are not equally reachable, and two of them pull against each other.

**Reports come first**, because they are the enabler. Links are the constraint on everything else. Without referring domains, the comparison pages never rank, the affiliate pages never convert, and the ClaryBook referral never happens. A price index is the cheapest link you will ever buy.

**ClaryBook traffic comes second**, and it is real but slow. Six to twelve months before it means anything. If you need signups this quarter, this project does not deliver them.

**Affiliate revenue comes last**, and it is smaller than it looks. Run the numbers below before you get attached to it.

## The conflict, and the one rule that resolves it

You are a bookkeeping software company about to publish rankings of bookkeeping software. There are two ways to handle that and only one of them works.

Hiding it fails. Your LLC registration is public, whois is public, the Railway deployment is traceable, and a competitor will find it the first week you rank for anything. The FTC's endorsement guides at 16 CFR 255 require disclosure of a material connection anyway, so the undisclosed version is both fragile and non-compliant.

So own it, loudly, from the first published page:

- **Site-wide disclosure above the fold**: built by West Hill Ventures LLC, which also builds ClaryBook. Same scoring method for every tool. ClaryBook is not ranked first anywhere on this site.
- **ClaryBook never takes the top slot** in a general ranking. Not once, not on a technicality.
- **Publish the method and the raw data.** Anyone can check the scoring.

This is not a handicap you accept, it is the position you sell. Forbes Advisor's ranking order is shaped by commercial relationships disclosed in a footnote nobody reads. Yours states the conflict in the header and hands over the spreadsheet. Say that on the methodology page, in those words. It is a better story than pretending to be independent, and it is the only version that survives being found out.

## How ClaryBook actually gets traffic from this

Not by being ranked first. By four mechanisms that all survive the rule above:

1. **Segment pages where ClaryBook honestly wins.** "Bookkeeping software for sole proprietors who will not open a spreadsheet." "Bookkeeping you can do from your phone." ClaryBook can top a segment it genuinely leads, because the claim holds up.
2. **Presence in every comparison a buyer reads.** Being one of thirty listed tools, with a fair writeup, in front of people actively shopping. That is the whole value of a directory listing and it costs nothing extra.
3. **Alternatives pages.** "QuickBooks Self-Employed alternatives" is a query with buying intent and far less competition than the head term. ClaryBook belongs on that list on merit.
4. **The reports carry the WHV name**, and ClaryBook is its sibling. Citations to the price index are brand surface for both.

## Affiliate: what it is for, and the rule that stops it eating ClaryBook

The numbers, so you can size it properly. QuickBooks pays around $88 per sale plus $8 per lead on a 45-day cookie. FreshBooks pays up to $200 per sale, one-time, on a 120-day cookie. Xero is roughly 10%. Clearing $2k a month needs somewhere around 20 to 25 conversions, which needs maybe 5,000 to 10,000 targeted sessions a month. That is a real site, a year or two out.

Now compare. A ClaryBook customer at $30/mo who stays a year is $360, recurring, on your own book. Roughly four times the QuickBooks payout, and it does not stop. Sending a sole proprietor to QuickBooks for $88 is a bad trade every single time.

So affiliate does two jobs, neither of which is "make money":
- It makes the site legible as a publisher. A site with no visible business model reads as a front, which is worse for trust than an honest affiliate disclosure.
- It pays the running costs.

**The rule that removes the conflict entirely:** split the catalog by whether ClaryBook competes.

| Page targets | Monetization |
|---|---|
| Sole props, 1099, freelancers, single-property landlords (ClaryBook's ICP) | CTA is ClaryBook. No competitor affiliate links on the page. |
| Inventory, payroll-heavy, multi-entity, job costing, firms with staff | Pure affiliate. ClaryBook is not listed. Recommend honestly, take the commission. |

Every page then knows which business it serves, and no page is ever quietly selling your own customer to a competitor for a one-time fee.

One practical note: most affiliate programs reject sites with no traffic. Do not apply in month one and collect rejections that are awkward to reverse. Apply around month four to six with real numbers, through Impact, PartnerStack or ShareASale.

## The reports: this is the best idea you had

Treat them as the main product. They are the only part of this that earns links, and links are the constraint.

Four concrete ones:

1. **Bookkeeping Software Price Index**, quarterly. Median list price across the tracked tools, plotted over time. Price rises are news, and news gets linked.
2. **Pricing change log.** Who raised prices, when, by how much. Nobody publishes this because nobody stores the history.
3. **Feature coverage matrix**, free CSV and JSON, CC BY licensed. Use it, cite us.
4. **One embeddable chart**, inline SVG with an attribution line. Bloggers embed, the link comes with it.

Why this earns links when a listicle does not: a listicle competes head-on with Forbes and loses. A price index has no competitor, because producing one requires having watched prices for a year. Accountants' blogs, small-business newsletters and finance journalists link to numbers they cannot produce themselves. That is the entire mechanism.

And once the pipeline runs, each quarter's report costs nothing. That is the part that matches your constraint.

## Submissions

The point of letting vendors submit is not content. It is backlinks and dataset growth.

The flow, with no human in it:
- A form posts a pull request against the dataset repo.
- An agent validates every claim against the vendor's own public pricing page, then merges or comments asking for a source.
- Nothing publishes unreviewed. Unverifiable claims get rejected with a reason.

The hook is "claim your listing." Vendors link to a page they control, from their own site. That is the flywheel, and it is the cheapest link acquisition available.

**Do not sell placement.** The moment paid placement touches ranking order, the transparency argument collapses and the reports stop being citable. If you ever want paid listings, they sit outside the ranking, clearly labeled. Not in v1.

## Build: git as the database

Minimal, and it hands you the price history for free.

- One repo, static site, Dockerfile plus nginx on Railway. Same pattern as westhillventures.com, already working.
- The dataset lives in the repo, one file per tool, JSON or YAML.
- A scheduled job re-reads each vendor's pricing page and commits what changed. **The git diff is the change log. The git log is the price history.** No database, no CMS, no admin panel.
- The report generator reads git log and emits the price index.
- Submissions arrive as pull requests against the same files.

Why this rather than a CMS: agents edit files well and click through admin panels badly, git gives an audit trail without building one, and the version history you need for the reports is precisely what git already is.

## Metrics and kill criteria

Written before it starts, per the GTM rules.

- **Day 90.** Dataset live, 30 or more tools, both reports published, site resolving. Miss this and the pipeline is the problem. Stop and fix the pipeline before writing another page.
- **Day 180.** 20 or more earned referring domains, 500 or more organic sessions a month. Under 5 referring domains and under 100 sessions means the report thesis is wrong. Redirect the domain to clarybook.com and keep whatever links exist.
- **Day 270.** 50 or more clicks a month through to ClaryBook, at least one attributed trial. Zero attributed trials at month nine means it does not serve goal one. Keep it only if affiliate revenue clears the running cost, otherwise kill it.

Cash exposure: the domain is already paid for, Railway is roughly $5 to $10 a month. Under $150 a year. The real cost is agent time, not money, which is why this clears the bar even at slow returns.

## Risks, named

1. **Google is hostile to this genre right now.** The 2026 core and spam updates hit thin affiliate comparison content hardest. The defense is that the price history is original data nobody else holds, and that every page states plainly which tools were used firsthand and which were assessed from public sources. If we have not tested it, the page says so. That is a ranking signal, not an admission.
2. **It is slow.** Six to twelve months to meaningful organic traffic. Not a fix for this quarter.
3. **Reputational.** "ClaryBook secretly runs a review site" is a story a competitor writes exactly once. The disclosure has to ship with the first page. It cannot be added later, because the story is about the concealment, not the ownership.
4. **Program rejection.** Affiliate networks turn down zero-traffic sites, which is why the application sits at month four, not month one.

## First move

Once the disclosure rule and the DNS are settled: build the dataset for 30 tools, ship the methodology page and the first price snapshot, and publish nothing else until there is data worth comparing. The pages come after the data, not before. Building pages first is how this turns into the thin affiliate site Google is currently deleting.
